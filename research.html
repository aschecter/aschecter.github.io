Research

Research

Computational social science at the intersection of people, teams, and AI.

My current research interests lie broadly in the domain of computational social science, with three complementary areas of emphasis. A unifying thread is the use of novel computational and statistical tools to understand how people, groups, and technologies interact over time.

Copies of any of the papers below are available on request — just email me.

Coordination in Large Online Groups

How do hundreds or thousands of loosely coupled contributors get anything done together? My work on this question focuses on open-source software projects, digital platforms, and open innovation communities — settings where formal authority is thin, participation is voluntary, and coordination has to emerge from the interaction patterns themselves. I use relational event models, network analysis, and mixture models to characterize how task allocation, knowledge brokerage, and core-periphery dynamics shape what these communities produce.

Representative work: Lindberg et al. (2024, MIS Quarterly) on the entrainment of task allocation cycles in open-source development; Mattarelli et al. (2025, Research Policy) on secondhand social capital and idea quality in open innovation communities; Brunswicker & Schecter (2019, Research Policy) on the paradox of change for developers’ digital innovation trajectories.

Human-AI Collaboration and the Valuation of AI-Augmented Work

As AI systems move from tools to teammates, the interesting questions stop being about whether algorithms outperform humans and start being about how the joint system actually behaves — when people accept advice, when they over-rely, how the presence of an AI collaborator changes how work is judged by outsiders, and how disclosure and attribution shape that judgment. I run experiments and field studies on these dynamics, with a particular focus on the gap between how creators evaluate AI-augmented work and how external evaluators do.

Representative work: Panda Kumar Sachin & Schecter (2024, JAIS) on advice utilization in human-algorithm decision-making; Bogert, Schecter, & Watson (2021, Scientific Reports) on algorithmic reliance and task difficulty; Schecter et al. (2023, Computers in Human Behavior) introducing Vero, a low-tech method for studying human-AI collaboration.

Methods for Sequential, Relational, and AI-Generated Data

The substantive questions in the first two streams kept running into methodological gaps, so I work on closing them. This stream develops and extends methods for analyzing relational event sequences (including mixture variants that recover latent behavioral archetypes), for doing valid statistical inference when key variables are themselves outputs of machine learning models, and for surfacing constructs from unstructured text in ways researchers can defend.

Representative work: Schecter, Nohadani, & Contractor (2022, MIS Quarterly) on robust inference for decision-making in networks; Schecter & Li (2026, Information Systems Research) on robust optimization for inference with ML-generated variables; Schecter & Quintane (2021, Organizational Research Methods) on the power and precision of the relational event model.

Featured Project

AI Spillovers & Digital Imprinting

Once an AI system is used at one stage of a workflow, its influence rarely stays put. A model’s output becomes a colleague’s input; an annotation becomes training data; a recommendation becomes a default.

With Benjamin Richardson and Panda Kumar Sachin, I’m tracing how these imprints propagate through algorithmic ecosystems — how appreciation, aversion, and trust generalize across systems, and what that means for organizations trying to govern AI use without locking themselves into the first model they tried.
